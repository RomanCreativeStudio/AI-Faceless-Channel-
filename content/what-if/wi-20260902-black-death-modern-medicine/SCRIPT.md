## Hook

"In 1347, a disease killed up to 60% of the people in some parts of
Europe — and nobody knew why. What if the people living through it had
known just a few of the things we know today?"

## Premise

Not "modern medicine saves the day" — a bounded question: how might the
Black Death have unfolded if select 14th-century communities had germ
theory, basic case-tracking, sanitation, and quarantine capability, but
still no antibiotics, vaccines, diagnostics, or hospitals?

## Narrative beats

1. **The known baseline** — what actually happened: 1347–1351, spread
   across Europe, mortality estimates from ~25% to 60%+ depending on
   region and study. — claims: `c1`
2. **Why nobody could stop it** — no germ theory existed anywhere in the
   world yet; disease was understood through humoral/religious
   frameworks, not microorganisms. — claims: `c2`
3. **The hypothetical, defined precisely** — name exactly what knowledge
   is being granted (germ theory, case tracking, sanitation, quarantine
   capability) and exactly what is withheld (antibiotics, vaccines,
   diagnostics, pharmaceutical manufacturing, hospitals). — claims: `c4`, `c12`
4. **What plausibly changes** — earlier identification and isolation of
   cases could reduce transmission chains; but anyone infected still
   faces historically-grounded fatality risk, because there is still no
   cure. — claims: `c3`, `c10`, `c11`, `c6`
5. **Where the hypothetical hits its limits** — pneumonic (airborne)
   plague is harder to contain than bubonic even with quarantine
   knowledge, given medieval enforcement limits. — claims: `c7`
6. **What we can't know** — the actual size of any reduction in deaths
   can't be estimated with confidence, and it's not even certain a
   germ-based model would have been widely trusted in a 14th-century
   worldview. — claims: `c8`, `c9`

## Transitions

1→2: "So what actually happened is well documented. What's less obvious

4→5: "But that's not the whole picture." 5→6: "And here's the part a
responsible answer has to admit."

## Conclusion

Selected modern knowledge, applied to the 14th century, plausibly changes
*how* the Black Death spread — not whether it was stopped. Without a cure,
people who caught it still faced the same grim odds. This isn't a story
about medicine saving the day; it's about which parts of "modern medicine"
actually matter, and which parts don't help without the rest.

## Uncertainty notes

Beat 5 (`c7`) and both claims in beat 6 (`c8`, `c9`) are `LOW` confidence
and must be delivered with explicit hedging language ("it's hard to say,"
"we can't know for sure") rather than assertive narration.

## What If? fact/hypothesis separation

- **KNOWN FACT** — The Black Death's actual timeline and mortality (`c1`);
  the total absence of germ theory in the 14th century and its later
  development in the 1860s–1880s (`c2`); untreated plague fatality rates
  (`c3`); modern antibiotic treatment efficacy (`c10`); antibiotics not
  existing until the 20th century (`c11`).
- **ASSUMPTION** — This scenario grants germ theory, case tracking,
  sanitation, and quarantine capability (`c4`); it withholds antibiotics,
  vaccines, diagnostics, pharmaceutical manufacturing, and hospitals
  (`c12`, successor to superseded `c5`).
- **INFERENCE** — Transmission chains could plausibly shrink, but
  infected individuals still face historical fatality risk absent a cure
  (`c6`); airborne pneumonic plague likely remains harder to contain than
  bubonic even with the granted knowledge (`c7`).
- **SPECULATION** — The actual size of any reduction in deaths is not
  estimable from available evidence (`c8`); whether a germ-based model
  would have been broadly trusted in a 14th-century worldview is unknown
  (`c9`).

The script never states c6–c9 as established outcomes; beats 4–6 use
hedged language throughout and beat 6 exists specifically to prevent the
piece from reading as "modern medicine would have solved it."
