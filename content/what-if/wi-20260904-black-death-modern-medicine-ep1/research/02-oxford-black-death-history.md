## Relevant evidence

- The Black Death struck in the mid-14th century, 1347–53 (peak impact
  commonly cited as 1347–1351).
- Current scholarly consensus favors rat-flea-borne bubonic plague as the
  cause, though this was debated from 2001 onward.
- Traditional estimates put mortality at 25–33% of Western Europe's
  population; more recent research puts it substantially higher — a 2010
  computer-assisted study found England's population fell 46% (4.8M to
  2.6M) in the first strike alone (1348–1351), with population down 60%
  from 1348 levels by 1450 and no recovery beginning until after 1500.
  Eastern Europe, the Middle East, and North Africa were struck at similar
  or slightly lower rates; evidence for major impact on China/India is
  weak.
- The plague was followed by roughly 30 further major epidemics through
  1720, and triggered lasting economic/social restructuring (shift from
  grain to pastoral agriculture, trade specialization, adoption of
  technologies like water mills and cannons) that helped enable European
  expansion from the 1490s.

## Conflicting evidence

Mortality estimates vary by methodology and era of scholarship (25–33%
traditional vs. 46–60%+ from post-1960 and post-2010 quantitative work).
Both figures are reported here rather than treated as a single number.
