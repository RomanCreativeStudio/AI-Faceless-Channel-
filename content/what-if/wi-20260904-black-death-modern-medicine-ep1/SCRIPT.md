## Hook

"In 1347, a disease killed up to 60% of the people in some parts of
Europe — and nobody knew why. What if the people living through it had
known just a few of the things we know today?"

## Premise

Not "modern medicine saves the day" — a bounded question: how might the
Black Death have unfolded if select 14th-century communities had germ
theory, basic case-tracking, sanitation, and quarantine capability, but
still no antibiotics, vaccines, diagnostics, or hospitals?

## Narrative beats

1. Between 1347 and 1351, the Black Death swept across Europe. Depending on the region and how historians measure it, somewhere between a quarter and well over half of the population died. In parts of England, the very first wave alone wiped out nearly half the people. — claims: `c1`
2. Nobody living through it understood why it was happening. The idea that specific microorganisms cause disease — germ theory — didn't exist anywhere in the world yet. That wouldn't be established until the second half of the 1800s, through the work of scientists like Pasteur, Lister, and Koch. In 1347, plague was explained through religion, or through the idea that bad air itself made people sick. — claims: `c2`
3. So here's the hypothetical. Let's say a handful of 14th-century communities somehow had four specific things: germ theory, so they understood disease spreads from person to person; basic case tracking, so they could see where it was spreading; sanitation practices, like managing waste and washing hands; and the practical ability to quarantine the sick. That's it. No antibiotics, no vaccines, no modern diagnostic tests, no pharmaceutical factories, and no hospitals as we'd recognize them today. Just those four ideas, dropped into a medieval world. — claims: `c4`, `c12`
4. What would that actually change? Earlier identification and isolation could plausibly slow how the disease spread from person to person. But here's the part that matters most: if you already had the plague, none of that would save you. Without treatment, bubonic plague killed an estimated thirty to sixty percent of the people it infected, and pneumonic plague was described as almost universally fatal. Modern antibiotics can treat plague effectively today, even in its worst forms — but antibiotics didn't exist yet. They wouldn't be developed until the 20th century. So this hypothetical could plausibly change how fast the disease moved. It could not change what happened to the people who caught it. — claims: `c3`, `c10`, `c11`, `c6`
5. And even the "how it spreads" part has a real limit. Pneumonic plague, the airborne form, spreads directly between people through the air. Even with quarantine knowledge, a 14th-century community's ability to actually enforce isolation was nowhere near what a modern public health system can do. So this form of the disease would likely have remained hard to contain, granted knowledge or not. — claims: `c7`
6. Here's what we genuinely don't know. Nobody can say with any confidence how many deaths this would have actually prevented — the numbers just aren't there to estimate it. And there's an even more basic question underneath that: would a 14th-century society, whose entire understanding of disease was religious and humoral, have even trusted or adopted a strange new idea about invisible organisms? We don't know that either. — claims: `c8`, `c9`

## Conclusion

Selected modern knowledge, applied to the 14th century, plausibly changes
*how* the Black Death spread — not whether it was stopped. Without a cure,
people who caught it still faced the same grim odds. This isn't a story
about medicine saving the day; it's about which parts of "modern medicine"
actually matter, and which parts don't help without the rest.

## What If? fact/hypothesis separation

- **KNOWN FACT** — The Black Death's actual timeline and mortality (`c1`);
  the total absence of germ theory in the 14th century and its later
  development in the 1860s–1880s (`c2`); untreated plague fatality rates
  (`c3`); modern antibiotic treatment efficacy (`c10`); antibiotics not
  existing until the 20th century (`c11`).
- **ASSUMPTION** — This scenario grants germ theory, case tracking,
  sanitation, and quarantine capability (`c4`); it withholds antibiotics,
  vaccines, diagnostics, pharmaceutical manufacturing, and hospitals
  (`c12`, successor to superseded `c5`).
- **INFERENCE** — Transmission chains could plausibly shrink, but
  infected individuals still face historical fatality risk absent a cure
  (`c6`); airborne pneumonic plague likely remains harder to contain than
  bubonic even with the granted knowledge (`c7`).
- **SPECULATION** — The actual size of any reduction in deaths is not
  estimable from available evidence (`c8`); whether a germ-based model
  would have been broadly trusted in a 14th-century worldview is unknown
  (`c9`).

The script never states c6–c9 as established outcomes; beats 4–6 use
hedged language throughout ("plausibly," "could not," "likely," "we
genuinely don't know") and beat 6 exists specifically to prevent the
piece from reading as "modern medicine would have solved it."
